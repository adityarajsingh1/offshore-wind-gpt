# Inside an offshore wind turbine

A typical offshore turbine is a three-bladed, upwind, horizontal-axis machine. The main parts are the rotor (blades and hub), the nacelle, the tower and the foundation.

The blades are made mainly of glass fibre or carbon fibre composites. They are shaped like aircraft wings: wind flowing over the blade creates lift, which turns the rotor. The pitch system rotates each blade around its own axis to control how much power is captured. In strong winds the blades are pitched to shed power, and in a storm they are feathered so the turbine stops safely. The cut-out wind speed for most turbines is around 25 to 30 metres per second.

The nacelle sits on top of the tower and holds the drivetrain: the main shaft, bearings, the generator and, in geared designs, the gearbox. Many large offshore turbines use direct-drive or medium-speed drivetrains with permanent magnet generators, because gearboxes are a common source of failures and repairs at sea are very expensive. The power converter and transformer are also in the nacelle or the tower base.

The yaw system turns the whole nacelle so the rotor faces into the wind. A wind vane and anemometer on the nacelle, and sometimes a nacelle-mounted lidar, measure the wind for the controller.

Every turbine is monitored through a SCADA system (supervisory control and data acquisition), which records data such as power, wind speed, temperatures and vibrations every few seconds. Operators use this data for condition monitoring and predictive maintenance, catching problems such as bearing wear before they cause a breakdown.
