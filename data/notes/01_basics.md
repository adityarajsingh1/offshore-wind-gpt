# Offshore wind basics

Offshore wind is electricity generated by wind turbines built at sea, usually on the continental shelf. Winds over the sea are generally stronger and steadier than on land because there are no hills, buildings or forests to slow the air down. Since the power in the wind rises with the cube of wind speed, a small increase in average wind speed gives a large increase in energy. Doubling the wind speed gives eight times the power.

Offshore turbines also avoid many of the problems of onshore sites. There is more space, so turbines can be much bigger, and they are usually far enough from homes that noise and visual impact matter less. The trade-off is cost and difficulty: everything has to be built, connected and maintained in a harsh marine environment, which needs special vessels, cables and foundations.

The capacity factor of a wind farm is the energy it actually produces in a year divided by what it would produce running at full power all year. Modern offshore wind farms in good locations such as the North Sea typically reach capacity factors of around 40 to 50 percent, noticeably higher than most onshore wind farms.

Betz's law sets a theoretical upper limit on how much energy any turbine can extract from the wind: 16/27, or about 59.3 percent. Real turbines reach roughly 75 to 80 percent of this limit at their best operating point.

The first offshore wind farm was Vindeby in Denmark, built in 1991 with eleven 450 kW turbines. It ran for about 25 years before being decommissioned in 2017. Since then turbines have grown enormously. Turbines installed in the 2020s are commonly rated at 12 to 15 MW, with rotor diameters of more than 200 metres, and prototypes above 20 MW have been built.
