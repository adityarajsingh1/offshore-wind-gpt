# Project lifecycle, costs and the environment

An offshore wind project usually goes through these stages: site selection and leasing, surveys (wind, metocean, geophysical and geotechnical), environmental impact assessment and consenting, design and procurement, construction and installation, operations and maintenance, and finally decommissioning or repowering. Developing a project from first idea to operation commonly takes seven to ten years. Wind farms are typically designed for an operating life of 25 to 30 years.

Metocean data describes the wind, waves, currents and water levels at the site. It drives the structural design and the weather windows for installation and maintenance. Significant wave height is a key measure: crew transfer vessels can usually only transfer technicians safely when it is below roughly 1.5 metres, while larger service operation vessels with motion-compensated gangways can work in rougher seas.

Installation uses specialised ships. Jack-up vessels stand on legs on the seabed to provide a stable platform for lifting turbine components with cranes that reach well over 100 metres high. Heavy-lift vessels install foundations and substations, and cable-laying vessels install the array and export cables.

Operations and maintenance (O&M) is a large share of lifetime cost. Smaller wind farms close to shore are usually serviced by crew transfer vessels from a port, while large far-offshore farms use service operation vessels that stay at sea for weeks with technicians living on board. Helicopters are also used for urgent access.

The levelized cost of energy (LCOE) is the average cost of producing each megawatt-hour over a project's life, including building, running and financing it. Offshore wind costs fell sharply during the 2010s thanks to bigger turbines, larger projects and competitive auctions, although inflation, higher interest rates and supply chain pressures pushed costs back up in the early 2020s. Many governments support projects through contracts for difference (CfDs), where the generator receives a fixed strike price: if the market price is lower, the generator is paid the difference, and if it is higher, the generator pays the difference back.

Environmental effects include underwater noise during construction, the risk of bird collisions, displacement of some seabirds, and changes to habitats. Foundations and scour protection can also act as artificial reefs that attract fish and shellfish. Projects go through environmental impact assessments and are often monitored before, during and after construction.

Europe built most of the early offshore wind, led by the UK, Germany and Denmark, with the North Sea as the main hub. China has since become the country with the largest installed offshore wind capacity. Other growing markets include Taiwan, South Korea, Japan, Vietnam, Australia and the United States.
