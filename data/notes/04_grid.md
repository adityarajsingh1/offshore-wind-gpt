# Getting the power to shore

Inside a wind farm, turbines are connected in strings by array cables, typically at 33 kV in older farms and 66 kV in newer ones. The array cables bring power to an offshore substation, where transformers step the voltage up, commonly to 132 kV to 275 kV for AC export. Export cables then carry the power to an onshore substation and into the grid.

High-voltage alternating current (HVAC) export works well for wind farms relatively close to shore. Over long distances, AC cables lose a lot of capacity to charging currents, so for farms far offshore, roughly beyond 80 to 100 km, high-voltage direct current (HVDC) becomes the better choice. HVDC needs a converter station offshore and another onshore, which are expensive, but the cable losses are much lower.

Subsea cables are one of the biggest risks in an offshore project. Cable faults, often caused by damage during installation, anchors or fishing gear, or seabed movement, are a leading cause of insurance claims in offshore wind. Cables are normally buried one to three metres below the seabed for protection.

Because wind output varies with the weather, grid operators rely on wind power forecasting, interconnectors between countries, flexible power plants and increasingly battery storage to balance the system. Some projects plan to use surplus offshore wind to make green hydrogen through electrolysis.
